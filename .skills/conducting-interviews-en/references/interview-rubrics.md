# Interview Rubrics

Use these rubrics to create structured interviews and evidence-based evaluations. Adapt competency names to the role instead of forcing every interview to use every category.

## General Competency Rubric

| Score | Meaning | Evidence Standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Exceptional | Clear, specific examples show repeated success at or above the target level, with strong judgment, ownership, and measurable impact. |
| 4 | Strong | Evidence shows the candidate can perform well at the target level with limited concerns. |
| 3 | Meets bar | Evidence supports baseline ability, but depth, scope, or consistency is not yet fully proven. |
| 2 | Below bar | Evidence is thin, indirect, or shows meaningful gaps for the role. |
| 1 | Clear miss | Evidence shows the candidate is unlikely to succeed in this competency at the required level. |
| N/A | Not assessed | The interview did not collect enough relevant evidence. |

## Common Competencies

### Role Knowledge

Strong evidence:

- Understands core domain concepts and tradeoffs.
- Explains prior work with precision.
- Connects decisions to user, business, technical, or operational impact.

Risk evidence:

- Stays abstract or generic.
- Cannot explain the why behind decisions.
- Relies mainly on team reputation or vague outcomes.

### Problem Solving

Strong evidence:

- Frames ambiguous problems clearly.
- Identifies constraints and assumptions.
- Compares options and tradeoffs.
- Adapts when new information appears.

Risk evidence:

- Jumps to solutions without diagnosis.
- Ignores constraints.
- Cannot explain why alternatives were rejected.

### Execution and Ownership

Strong evidence:

- Shows clear personal ownership.
- Describes obstacles and how they were handled.
- Tracks outcomes and follow-through.
- Escalates or collaborates appropriately.

Risk evidence:

- Uses "we" without clarifying personal contribution.
- Blames others without learning.
- Cannot describe measurable results.

### Communication

Strong evidence:

- Explains complex topics clearly.
- Adjusts detail level to the audience.
- Uses examples and structure.
- Handles disagreement constructively.

Risk evidence:

- Gives hard-to-follow answers.
- Avoids direct answers.
- Over-indexes on jargon.

### Collaboration

Strong evidence:

- Builds alignment across functions.
- Handles conflict with maturity.
- Gives credit and describes shared decision-making.
- Understands stakeholder incentives.

Risk evidence:

- Shows low empathy for other functions.
- Frames disagreement as incompetence.
- Cannot describe how alignment was reached.

### Leadership

Strong evidence:

- Raises the quality of the team.
- Makes decisions under uncertainty.
- Coaches others or creates operating leverage.
- Owns outcomes beyond personal tasks.

Risk evidence:

- Confuses authority with leadership.
- Avoids hard calls.
- Cannot describe team-level impact.

### Customer or Product Sense

Strong evidence:

- Starts from user or customer problems.
- Defines success metrics.
- Balances user value, business value, feasibility, and risk.
- Uses evidence to prioritize.

Risk evidence:

- Optimizes for opinion or novelty.
- Cannot identify the target user.
- Ignores adoption, usability, or business constraints.

### Technical Depth

Strong evidence:

- Explains architecture, implementation details, tradeoffs, and failure modes.
- Understands debugging and operational concerns.
- Can reason from fundamentals.

Risk evidence:

- Only describes surface-level involvement.
- Cannot explain constraints or edge cases.
- Avoids specifics.

## Behavioral Question Pattern

Use this pattern for behavioral interviews:

1. Ask for a specific past example.
2. Clarify context and constraints.
3. Ask what the candidate personally did.
4. Ask what alternatives they considered.
5. Ask what result happened.
6. Ask what they learned or would do differently.

Example follow-ups:

- What was your role versus the team's role?
- What was hard about this situation?
- What options did you consider?
- How did you know it worked?
- What changed after this work?
- What would you do differently now?

## Technical or Case Interview Rubric

| Area | Strong Signal | Risk Signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem framing | Clarifies goals, users, constraints, and success criteria. | Starts building before understanding the problem. |
| Reasoning | Makes tradeoffs explicit and adjusts with new information. | Treats assumptions as facts. |
| Depth | Handles edge cases, scaling, failure modes, and implementation details. | Stays high level after follow-up probes. |
| Communication | Explains choices clearly and invites clarification. | Produces opaque answers or jumps around. |
| Practicality | Chooses realistic solutions for the role and context. | Over-engineers or ignores feasibility. |

## Interview Plan Template

| Segment | Time | Purpose | Prompt |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | 3 min | Set context and reduce ambiguity. | "I will ask about specific examples from your past work and leave time for your questions." |
| Role context | 5 min | Understand relevant background. | "Which parts of your recent work are most relevant to this role?" |
| Competency 1 | 10 min | Assess a core requirement. | Use a behavioral or technical question. |
| Competency 2 | 10 min | Assess a second requirement. | Use a behavioral or technical question. |
| Deep dive | 10 min | Test ownership and judgment. | Follow up on the strongest example. |
| Candidate questions | 7 min | Let the candidate evaluate fit. | Answer honestly and note candidate priorities. |
| Close | 5 min | Explain next steps. | "Thank you. The team will follow up with next steps." |

## Debrief Template

### Summary

State the role, interview type, candidate evidence level, and recommendation.

### Strengths

List only strengths supported by notes.

### Risks

List role-relevant risks and missing evidence.

### Evidence Table

| Claim | Evidence | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  |  | Low / Medium / High |

### Recommendation

Use one of:

- Strong yes: strong evidence across critical competencies, low unresolved risk.
- Yes: meets the bar with manageable risks.
- Leaning yes: promising but needs one or two follow-ups.
- Mixed: meaningful evidence on both sides; decision needs calibration.
- Leaning no: gaps likely outweigh strengths for this role.
- No: clear evidence below the bar on critical competencies.

Always include confidence level and next step.

## Unsafe Question Guardrails

Avoid questions about:

- Age, graduation year, or retirement plans.
- Marital status, childcare, pregnancy, or family plans.
- Disability, medical history, mental health, or sick leave history.
- Religion, political affiliation, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or gender identity.
- Nationality or citizenship, except neutral work-authorization questions when legally required.
- Salary history where restricted.
- Arrest history or protected legal history unless required and handled by HR/legal policy.

Safer rewrites:

| Risky Question | Safer Version |
| --- | --- |
| "Do you have kids? Can you handle late nights?" | "This role occasionally requires work outside standard hours. Are you able to meet that requirement?" |
| "Where are you originally from?" | "Are you authorized to work in the location required for this role?" |
| "How old are you?" | Remove the question. It is not role-relevant. |
| "Do you have any health issues?" | "Can you perform the essential functions of the role, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |
| "What is your current salary?" | "What compensation range are you targeting for this role?" |
